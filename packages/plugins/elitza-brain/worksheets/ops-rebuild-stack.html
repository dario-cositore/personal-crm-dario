Part 1 · Find the Break in Your Workflow in One Sitting

I run this intake at the start of every repair engagement. You get the same fillable workbook: inventory, failure signals, scoring, and a ranked fix list you can hand to any developer or agency.

This is the intake I run at the start of every repair, packaged as something you can run yourself. No theory, no prompt engineering hacks: just operational reality. Work the sections in order. The order is the diagnosis.

Before you start

Broken workflow automation fails quietly. The form fires, the CRM receives the contact, sales sees the name. Everything looks alive. Then the contact has no source, the deal never opens, and the owner field stays blank. Nobody notices until the follow-up window has closed.

The failure is rarely in the model. It is in the wiring, the retries and the missing audit trail. This kit finds the wiring.

Ground rules: audit the money path first. Lead intake, lead scoring, lead routing, deal creation, follow-up, payment handoff, onboarding, reporting. Everything else waits. Take the inventory before you change anything. Write down what you find while it is still true.

Section 3 · Failure signals that dashboards never show

Twelve patterns I find over and over. Tick what you can prove is happening, then carry the ticks into Section 4. Each line tells you where to look: the signal is rarely where the pain shows up.

1. Silent webhook failures: the event never arrives and nothing pages anyone. Where to look: endpoint logs, provider delivery history, any step with no failure handler.
2. Duplicate writes: the same contact exists three times with different owners, and every report downstream is wrong. Where to look: creation logic with no search-before-create, form resubmits, retry storms.
3. Missing idempotency: a replayed event creates a second record instead of updating the first. Where to look: no idempotency_key, no event_id stored, no raw payload archive.
4. Stale state: lifecycle stage sits in New for three days while the deal never opens. Where to look: stage transitions with no logger, filters that stop a path silently.
5. No audit trail: nobody can say what ran, what changed, or who approved it. Where to look: missing correlation_id, no before and after values, run history past retention.
6. Permission drift: a token expired, a scope narrowed, or an API key was rotated while the workflow kept calling. Where to look: 401 and 403 responses buried in module logs, service accounts with no owner.
7. Blank owner assignment: the record exists, the owner field stays empty, nothing routes. Where to look: routing rules keyed on a field the form never sends.
8. Dead owner IDs: a user left, the CRM kept the ID alive, leads land in an inbox nobody reads. Where to look: owner status checks in the routing layer.
9. Field map drift: a property was renamed six months ago and the chain still writes the old name, the old pipeline ID, the old trigger condition. Where to look: any scenario built months ago with no audit since.
10. Retries that swallow the error: the chain stops at step four, retries twice, then gives up quietly. Where to look: ignore-error flags, modules with no error handler, filters doing the job of error handling.
11. Filters as lead graveyards: one missing field blocks a good record and no queue ever holds it. Where to look: every filter sitting between a lead and the CRM.
12. Unowned automation chains: nobody has audited or tested them, and they run every day. Where to look: anything with no owner, no purpose, no last-tested date.

The five-step procedure I run on a scoped rebuild

Every intake runs the same priority order. The order is the diagnosis: fix the layer that silently breaks everything above it, before touching anything visible.

STEP 1The trigger and the payload

What I check: does the event fire, with the fields the next step actually expects?

Evidence to collect: one raw payload per source, the provider delivery log, the schema you believe is correct.

Pass condition: required fields present, types correct, event_id and correlation_id on every message, consent captured where it matters.

Common failure: a form label changed, the payload shape drifted, one required field came through empty and the chain ran anyway.

STEP 2The identity layer

What I check: dedupe rules, matching keys and owner assignment before anything writes.

Evidence to collect: search results for the last 20 records, the idempotency key format, the owner resolution rule.

Pass condition: search contact by email and company by domain before create, one idempotency key per source event, owner resolves to an active user every time.

Common failure: creation with no search, inactive owner IDs still sitting in the routing rule.

STEP 3The write path

What I check: schemas, permissions and idempotency on every CRM update.

Evidence to collect: the field map, token scopes and expiry, rate limit behaviour, raw API response codes.

Pass condition: every write validates against a schema, every retry is safe to replay, every permission is scoped and owned.

Common failure: renamed property, expired token, a retry that creates a second record instead of updating.

STEP 4The failure path

What I check: retries, dead-letter handling and alerting, so the next break announces itself instead of hiding.

Evidence to collect: an error handler on every module, the contents of any dead-letter queue, where alerts are routed.

Pass condition: every failure lands somewhere visible, with its payload, its correlation ID and a named owner.

Common failure: ignore-error flags, filters used as error handling, alerts sent to a channel nobody watches.

STEP 5The audit trail

What I check: a log of what ran, what changed and who approved it.

Evidence to collect: correlation ID threaded end to end, before and after values on each write, approval records on high-value changes.

Pass condition: any record can be reconstructed from its event_id, including the raw payload and the rule decision that produced it.

Common failure: run history past retention, no raw payload archive, no approval record on anything that touched money or a client.

Section 6 · FAQ

Do I need anyone on the call to run this?

No. You submit the stack and the symptoms, or you work the worksheets yourself. A conversation only happens when you ask for one.

Which workflows do I audit first?

The ones touching money. Lead capture, lead scoring, lead routing, deal creation, follow-up, payment handoff, onboarding, reporting. Everything else waits.

What stacks does it cover?

Make.com, Zapier, n8n, HubSpot, Salesforce, GoHighLevel, Clio, MyCase, Airtable, webhooks and custom APIs. If it moves data between two systems, it is in scope.

What do I walk away with?

A completed inventory, a ticked failure checklist, a ranked fix list in four tiers with the reasoning behind each ranking, and a triage lane for each fix: quick fix or full rebuild.

Can you implement the fixes too?

Yes. The diagnosis stands alone. Implementation runs through the AI workflow automation engagement. When the break is the data layer itself, the CRM Automation Repair Kit is the matching fix sheet.

What if the break sits between two business systems?

That usually belongs to a CRM and API workflow overhaul rather than a single workflow. The kit still maps it, and the ranking tells you where the boundary is.

Part 2 · CRM Automation Rebuild: Dedupe, Routing and SLA in One Pass

The rebuild I ran for a three office firm using a fraction of its CRM. You get dedupe, merge rules, routing reasons, timers and the audit trail that shows what every workflow actually did.

This is the repair kit for the layer between your forms, your CRM and your follow-up. Duplicate records, blind creates, owner assignment nobody can explain, no timer when a lead ages. The rebuild I ran for Meridian Legal Group started there: three office locations, twelve intake channels, a HubSpot data graveyard, duplicate contacts in the thousands and no SLA timers at all. Before you start

Most duplicate problems come from lazy creation logic. Create contact. Create deal. Send alert. Move on. Fast build, dirty future. A serious layer works in the opposite order. Capture the event. Normalize identifiers. Search existing records. Score possible matches. Update the right record. Create only when safe. Associate the right company, deal, note and owner. Store the source event. Log the decision.

Ground rules: take the inventory before you change anything. Find every path that can create a contact, a company or a deal. Forms, ad leads, calendar bookings, payment events, imports, old Zapier and Make flows, partner feeds. Then stop the sources before you clean the pile. Cleanup without stopping the source is a treadmill.

Identity normalization

Do not search raw form data. Raw form data lies. Email can include spaces and casing differences. Phone can arrive in five formats. Domain can include protocol, paths, subdomains and tracking junk. Normalize first, store both values.

email_normalized    = lower(trim(email))
phone_normalized    = e164(phone)
domain_normalized   = root_domain(url)      # strip protocol, www, path, utm
source_record_id    = provider + ":" + record_id
external_customer_id = stripe_customer_id | platform_customer_id
name_key            = lower(replace(full_name, " ", "_"))

raw values  -> stored as evidence, never used for matching
normalized  -> drives matching and idempotency

Search-before-create

This rule pays for itself immediately. Before creating a contact, search by normalized email. If no match, search by normalized phone. If no match, search by known external IDs. If no match, search by company domain with the person name as a candidate only. Then decide.

OrderMatch typeConfidenceActionNotes
0idempotency_key1.00Return previous result if processedReplay safety
1exact_email1.00Update existing contactPrimary key
2exact_phone0.92Update if no conflictE.164 form
3external_id1.00Update existing contactStripe, Calendly, Typeform
4company_domain + name0.74Manual review candidateNever auto-merge
5fuzzy name + company0.55Watch flag, new recordLow confidence

Exact matches can update automatically. Ambiguous matches go to review. No match creates a new record. That is the whole decision tree, and it is what stops blind creation.

Idempotency keys

Webhooks retry. Forms resubmit. Payment tools replay. CRMs fire update events after creation. Build a key from stable event data and store it.

{
  "event_type": "lead.submitted",
  "source_system": "typeform",
  "source_record_id": "tf_resp_01HYY4K8Q2",
  "idempotency_key": "contact_dedupe:typeform:tf_resp_01HYY4K8Q2:ops@example.com",
  "correlation_id": "corr_dedupe_01HYY4K8Q2",
  "received_at": "2026-04-26T21:08:19.219Z",
  "raw_payload_stored": true
}

If the key already succeeded, return the previous CRM object ID. Do not write again. This single rule removes the easiest class of duplicates: the second copy created by a retry storm.

Deals and ownership

Before creating a deal, search for open deals on the contact or company in the same pipeline. If one exists, update its context instead of opening another.
Preserve the existing owner on update unless a higher-priority routing rule overrides it. Owner churn is how follow-up history becomes unreadable.
Attach the source event as a note on the surviving record. The record should answer where it came from without opening a log.
Score leads only after identity resolves. Three contacts for one buyer means three partial scores and a wrong route.
Contact dedupe alone does not clean the pipeline. Duplicate deals are worse than duplicate contacts because they poison pipeline value directly. Search open deals before creating opportunities, every time.

Section 2 · Merge rules and conflict resolution

Automation should not merge records from weak evidence. Merge only from strong evidence. Medium confidence waits. Low confidence creates a new record with a watch flag. That gives you speed without a merge that destroys context.

Merge decision bands

BandConfidenceEvidence requiredAction
Auto merge0.90 to 1.00Exact email, exact external ID, or phone plus matching nameUpdate survivor, archive loser, write merge log
Review0.55 to 0.89Domain plus name, same phone different name, similar name same companyOpen candidate task, hold writes
Watchbelow 0.55Weak signal onlyCreate new record with watch flag, no merge

The review band is the one you tune. Move the lower bound up when reviewers keep rejecting auto-suggested merges. Move it down only when you can show the reviewer queue is reading correct suggestions.

Section 4 · SLA timers and the escalation ladder

Before the rebuild, Meridian Legal Group had no SLA timers at all. Leads aged quietly. Partners found out on the weekly report. A timer is not a notification. It is state attached to the record that escalates on its own when nobody acts.

Timer rules

Timer state lives with the record, not with the workflow run. A scenario restart must not reset the clock.
Business hours only for client-facing escalation, configurable per lane. After-hours clocks do not page people for a form fill at 23:40.
First response is measured against the assigned owner, not the queue. Owning a lead starts the clock.
Reassignment restarts the response window for the new owner and writes a second reason. The original breach stays recorded.
Every escalation writes an immutable event. You can reconstruct who was asked to act and when.

Three step escalation ladder

Three steps. Each step names who gets asked, what channel, and what happens if the step expires. The Meridian rebuild used the two hour and four hour steps below as its enforcement points.

Retry, backoff and dead letter

Workflows fail. The question is where the failure lands. Every write path needs the same three answers: how it retries, how it backs off, and where it goes when retries are exhausted.

FailurePolicyBackoffAfter max attempts
CRM search rate limitedRetry with backoffExponential with jitterDead letter queue
Multiple exact matchesManual review, no retryNoneReview queue
Missing email and phoneManual review, no retryNoneReview queue
API auth failureAlert operator, stop writesNoneOperator queue
Transient 5xxRetryExponential with jitterDead letter queue
Schema validation failOne corrected retryImmediateDead letter queue
Max attempts set and stored on the event. Five is a sane ceiling for CRM writes. Cap it, do not hope.
Exponential backoff with jitter, not a tight retry loop. A tight loop against a 429 is how you earn a longer ban.
Dead letter queue named and owned. crm_dedupe_failed_events in the sample payload below. Every item keeps its raw payload and correlation ID.

Section 6 · FAQ

Do I need to replace my CRM?

No. The layer sits between your sources and the CRM you already pay for. HubSpot, Salesforce, GoHighLevel, Clio, MyCase. The rules change with the API, not with the logo.

What if I already have thousands of duplicates?

Stop the sources first. Then clean in batches, starting with records tied to active pipeline. High-value contacts next, recent leads next. Records with conflicting owners, conflicting email or phone, or payment history go to review. Protect revenue history before vanity cleanliness.

Why not just auto-merge everything that looks close?

Because a bad merge destroys context you cannot rebuild. Attribution splits, ownership confuses, payment history goes missing. Merge only from strong evidence. Review the middle band. That is the whole policy.

What exactly is a routing reason?

A structured object and a readable sentence written onto the record at assignment time. Which rules matched, what they weighed, who was assigned, and what the tie break did. Sales can read it without asking ops.

How long before the SLA ladder matters?

Immediately if you have leads ageing without anyone noticing. The Meridian rebuild went from no timers at all to enforced first response with three steps. The timers are what made the rest of the rebuild visible.
